Affogato

Vanilla gelato "drowned" in a hot shot of espresso: dessert and coffee in one cup.

Ask staff about allergens

More details

Where it comes from

Affogato means "drowned" in Italian, and that is exactly what happens: a scoop of gelato disappears under a shot of hot espresso. It belongs to the Italian tradition of simple desserts built from very few ingredients, and in Italy it is found both at the bar and at the end of a meal in restaurants. Its appeal lies in contrast: hot and cold, bitter and sweet, liquid and creamy, all in the same cup.

How it is made

A scoop of vanilla gelato is placed in a cup or small glass, and an espresso is poured over it just before serving. The heat melts the edges of the gelato, which blends with the coffee into a creamy sauce, while the centre stays cold for a little longer. Timing matters: it is put together at the last moment and eaten straight away.

Curiosities

  • Vanilla and fior di latte are the classic flavours, because they let the coffee speak.
  • Some Italians add a splash of liqueur, in the same spirit as the caffè corretto, the espresso "corrected" with a spirit.

How to enjoy it

Eat it with a spoon straight away, before the gelato melts completely. Take a little gelato and a little coffee in each spoonful, and finish with the sweet coffee left at the bottom of the cup.

Americano

Espresso lengthened with hot water: a longer, lighter cup that keeps the espresso aroma.

Ask staff about allergens

More details

Where it comes from

The Americano is espresso stretched with hot water to come closer to the long, filtered coffee many visitors were used to. The most common story links its name to American soldiers in Italy during the Second World War, who found espresso too strong and asked for it to be diluted. Whether or not the anecdote is exact, the name stuck, and today the Americano is on the menu of cafés all over the world.

How it is made

A shot of espresso is extracted as usual, then combined with hot water in a larger cup. The water softens the intensity and makes the drink longer, while the espresso keeps its roasted aroma. Some baristas add the water first and pour the espresso on top to preserve part of the crema; others do the opposite.

Curiosities

  • In Italy it is sometimes confused with the caffè lungo, which is an espresso extracted with more water through the coffee itself, not diluted afterwards.
  • "Americano" is also the name of a classic Italian aperitivo cocktail, a completely different drink.

How to enjoy it

Take your time with it: it is meant to be sipped. Drink it black to taste the coffee, or add a splash of milk if you prefer it rounder.

Caffè Al Banco

An espresso the Italian way: ordered, drunk and enjoyed standing at the counter.

Ask staff about allergens

More details

Where it comes from

In Italy, "un caffè" means an espresso, and the most natural place to drink it is standing at the bar counter. The ritual grew with the espresso machine in the first half of the twentieth century: a quick stop on the way to work, a few words with the barista, a small cup finished in a couple of sips. The bar became a daily meeting point in every Italian town, from the city centre to the smallest village.

How it is made

The coffee is the same as a classic espresso: finely ground coffee, hot water pushed through it under pressure, a short and dense cup with a layer of crema on top. What changes is the moment. You order at the counter, the cup arrives on its saucer and you drink it right there; in Italy it often comes with a small glass of water on the side.

Curiosities

  • In many Italian bars a coffee at the counter costs less than the same coffee at a table, because standing is the everyday way to have it.
  • In Naples there is the custom of the caffè sospeso: paying for an extra coffee that a stranger can later claim for free.
  • Italians rarely linger over an espresso at the bar; a few minutes is the norm.

How to enjoy it

Stand at the counter, stir in sugar if you like, and drink it while it is hot and the crema is intact. It is a small break, meant to be short.

Caffè leccese

Espresso over ice with almond syrup: the summer coffee of Lecce and Salento.

Ask staff about allergens

More details

Where it comes from

Caffè leccese takes its name from Lecce, the baroque city at the heart of Salento, the southern tip of Puglia. Summers there are long and hot, and coffee over ice, caffè in ghiaccio, is an everyday habit in local bars. The leccese version adds almond, one of the best-known crops of Puglia, turning an iced espresso into a sweet, nutty drink that has become a symbol of summer in Salento.

How it is made

A glass is filled with ice and almond syrup is poured in. An espresso goes on top, and the drink is stirred so that the hot coffee cools down on the ice and blends with the almond. The traditional version uses latte di mandorla, a sweet almond preparation from the region; the result is cold, smooth and gently sweet.

Curiosities

  • It has become known well beyond Salento, and many bars elsewhere in Italy now offer it in summer.
  • In Salento the plain caffè in ghiaccio, espresso over ice with a little sugar, is just as common.
  • Puglia's almond tradition also shows in its sweets, such as pasta di mandorla.

How to enjoy it

Stir it well before the first sip so the almond reaches the whole glass. Drink it while the ice is still firm, ideally on a warm afternoon.

Caffellatte

Espresso with plenty of hot milk: the mild, milky coffee of the Italian breakfast table.

Ask staff about allergens

More details

Where it comes from

Caffellatte, literally "coffee milk", is one of the oldest ways Italians combine coffee and milk. At home it was the breakfast drink of many families: coffee from the stovetop pot poured into a bowl or large cup of hot milk, with bread or biscuits dipped in it. The bar version kept the same spirit, a big and mild coffee to start the day, made with espresso instead of home-brewed coffee.

How it is made

A shot of espresso is combined with a generous amount of hot milk, usually in a glass or a large cup. Compared with a cappuccino there is more milk and much less foam, so the drink is smoother and lighter in coffee taste, with the milk clearly in the lead.

Curiosities

  • In Italy, ordering "un latte" gets you a glass of milk; to get coffee as well you need the full word, caffellatte.
  • The international "latte" is a shortened form of this Italian name.
  • The Italian caffellatte usually has little or no foam, unlike many latte versions abroad.

How to enjoy it

Drink it slowly, while it is warm. It is a gentle choice for breakfast, or for anyone who likes their coffee soft and milky.

Cappuccino

Espresso crowned with silky steamed milk and foam, the classic Italian breakfast cup.

Ask staff about allergens

More details

Where it comes from

The cappuccino is one of the symbols of the Italian bar. Its name is usually linked to the Capuchin friars, whose brown habits recall the colour of coffee lightened with milk. In Vienna a coffee with cream called Kapuziner was already known in the nineteenth century; the cappuccino as we know it took shape in Italy in the twentieth century, when espresso machines with steam wands made it possible to foam milk at the counter.

How it is made

A cappuccino starts with a shot of espresso in a warm cup. Milk is heated and aerated with the steam wand until it turns glossy and creamy, then poured over the coffee so that the foam settles on top. The balance between coffee, milk and foam is what separates a cappuccino from a caffellatte, which has more milk and less foam.

Curiosities

  • In Italy the cappuccino is above all a morning drink, often paired with a cornetto; ordering one after lunch or dinner is unusual there.
  • Latte art, the hearts and rosettas drawn in the foam, depends on well-textured milk poured at the right moment.

How to enjoy it

Drink it while the foam is still airy, on its own or with breakfast. If you prefer a stronger coffee note, ask for an extra shot.

Cortado

Espresso "cut" with a little warm milk: short, balanced and smooth.

Ask staff about allergens

More details

Where it comes from

The cortado comes from Spain, where "cortar" means "to cut". A café cortado is an espresso cut with a small amount of warm milk, which softens its edges without hiding the coffee. It is a fixture of Spanish bars, has relatives across the Spanish- and Portuguese-speaking world, and has since become a regular in cafés far beyond.

How it is made

A shot of espresso is topped with roughly the same amount, or slightly less, of warm steamed milk, with little or no foam. The result is a small drink that sits between an espresso macchiato and a flat white in strength and size. The milk takes the sharpness off the espresso and adds a rounder, sweeter note.

Curiosities

  • It is close to the Italian espresso macchiato, but with more milk and less foam.
  • In Spain it is often drunk in the afternoon, after lunch, as a small pick-me-up.
  • Some Spanish cafés serve it in a small glass rather than a cup.

How to enjoy it

Drink it straight away, while the milk and coffee are still warm together. It is a good middle ground when an espresso feels too sharp and a cappuccino too big.

Doppio Espresso

Two shots of espresso in one cup: the same crema and aroma, with twice the coffee.

Ask staff about allergens

More details

Where it comes from

Doppio is simply Italian for "double". In Italy you might ask for "un caffè doppio" when a single espresso is not enough, but the word travelled much further abroad, where it became a standard name on café menus. It belongs to the same espresso tradition that grew in Italian bars through the twentieth century.

How it is made

A doppio uses a double filter basket holding twice the ground coffee of a single shot. Hot water is pushed through it under pressure, just like a regular espresso, and the result is a double volume of coffee with its layer of crema. Many drinks built on espresso, from flat whites to larger milk coffees, start from a double shot.

Curiosities

  • In Italy the single espresso is by far the most common order; a doppio is the exception rather than the rule.
  • A doppio is not the same as a lungo: a lungo pulls more water through a single dose, while a doppio doubles the coffee itself.
  • A portafilter with two spouts lets the barista split a double extraction into two cups or collect it all in one.

How to enjoy it

Treat it as a bigger espresso: drink it hot, while the crema lasts. It is a good choice for a long morning that needs more than a single shot.

Espresso

A short, intense cup with a layer of crema: the heart of every Italian bar.

Ask staff about allergens

More details

Where it comes from

Espresso was born in Italy between the late nineteenth and the early twentieth century, when inventors in Turin and Milan built machines that pushed hot water and steam through ground coffee to serve a cup in moments. After the Second World War, lever machines reached much higher pressure, and the dense, golden layer of crema appeared on top of the cup. From then on espresso became the base of almost every Italian coffee drink.

How it is made

Finely ground coffee is pressed into a filter basket, and hot water is forced through it at high pressure for a short time. The result is a small, concentrated cup with a full body and a hazel-coloured crema. Grind, dose, pressure and timing all have to be in balance: a small change shifts the taste towards bitter or sour.

Curiosities

  • The name is often explained as coffee made "expressly" for you, on the spot.
  • In Italy you simply ask for "un caffè"; the word espresso is used far more often abroad.
  • A ristretto is a shorter extraction of the same dose, a lungo a longer one.

How to enjoy it

Drink it soon after it is served, while the crema is still intact. Stir it once, add sugar if you like, and finish it in a few sips, as Italians do at the bar.

Espresso Macchiato

Espresso "stained" with a spoonful of milk foam: all the coffee, with a softer finish.

Ask staff about allergens

More details

Where it comes from

Macchiato means "stained" or "marked" in Italian. The espresso macchiato is an espresso marked with a little milk, and the name is often explained as the barista's way of telling a plain coffee apart from one with a touch of milk. It sits between the pure espresso and the cappuccino, and in Italian bars it is usually ordered simply as "un macchiato".

How it is made

A regular espresso is pulled into a small cup and topped with a small amount of steamed milk and foam, just enough to leave a pale spot on the crema. The coffee stays in the foreground; the milk only rounds off its intensity. In Italy you can ask for it "caldo", with hot foamed milk, or "freddo", with a drop of cold milk.

Curiosities

  • It is the mirror image of the latte macchiato, which is milk marked by coffee.
  • Unlike the cappuccino, many Italians drink a macchiato at any hour, after lunch included.
  • Outside Italy "macchiato" can also mean larger, sweeter milk drinks; the Italian original is small and short.

How to enjoy it

Drink it right away, like an espresso, in a few sips. It suits anyone who likes the strength of espresso but wants a slightly softer edge.

Latte Macchiato

Steamed milk "stained" with a shot of espresso: soft, milky and gently layered.

Ask staff about allergens

More details

Where it comes from

Latte macchiato means "stained milk": warm milk marked by a little coffee. It belongs to the Italian breakfast tradition, where a glass or cup of warm milk is a familiar sight, and a splash of espresso gives it a gentle coffee note. The name is the mirror image of the espresso macchiato, which is coffee marked by a little milk. Outside Italy it became popular as a milder, milkier alternative to the cappuccino.

How it is made

Milk is heated and foamed with the steam wand and poured into a glass. The espresso is added afterwards, slowly, so that it settles between the hot milk and the foam. Because milk, coffee and foam have different densities, the drink often shows distinct layers: milk at the bottom, a band of coffee in the middle and a cap of foam on top.

Curiosities

  • It is milder than a cappuccino, with more milk in proportion to the coffee.
  • Outside Italy the word "latte" alone usually means a coffee with milk; in Italy it simply means milk, so the full name matters.

How to enjoy it

Look at the layers first, then stir them together or sip your way down through them. It is a soft, milky coffee for a slower moment of the day.

Cioccolata calda

A cup of hot chocolate, the Italian cold-weather classic, to sip slowly.

Ask staff about allergens

More details

Where it comes from

Chocolate reached Europe from the Americas through Spain in the sixteenth century, first as a drink. In Italy it found a second home in Turin, where the court of the House of Savoy and the city's cafés made hot chocolate fashionable, and where chocolate making grew into a lasting craft. From there the habit of a cup of hot chocolate spread across the peninsula, especially in the colder months.

How it is made

Italian cioccolata calda is traditionally denser than the hot cocoa many people know from elsewhere. Chocolate or cocoa is heated with milk and stirred, often with a little starch, until the drink becomes thick and smooth enough to coat a spoon. It is served hot in a cup, sometimes topped with whipped cream.

Curiosities

  • In Turin the bicerin combines hot chocolate, espresso and cream in layers in a small glass.
  • Italian hot chocolate can be so thick that it is eaten with a spoon as much as it is drunk.
  • In many Italian bars it is a winter item that appears on the menu as soon as the weather turns cold.

How to enjoy it

Take it slowly, while it is still hot. It is a small comfort for a grey afternoon, on your own or shared with someone.

Latte e menta

Milk and mint syrup: a sweet, pale green classic of Italian bars.

Ask staff about allergens

More details

Where it comes from

Latte e menta is one of the simplest drinks of the Italian bar: milk with a splash of mint syrup. For decades it has been a summer classic in Italy, ordered by children and grown-ups alike when the day felt too hot for coffee. Mint syrup has long had its place on the shelves of Italian bars, next to orzata and the other syrups used to flavour water and milk.

How it is made

Mint syrup is poured into a glass and topped with milk, then stirred until the colour turns an even pale green. In Italy it is usually made with cold milk. The proportions are a matter of taste: a little syrup gives a delicate mint note, more syrup a sweeter and more intense drink.

Curiosities

  • The same syrup is used for acqua e menta, mint syrup with water, another classic of Italian summers.
  • Orzata, an almond syrup, is its close cousin on the bar shelf and is mixed with milk or water in the same way.
  • For many Italians it has a nostalgic ring, tied to summer holidays and beach bars.

How to enjoy it

Stir it once more before drinking, so the syrup does not settle at the bottom. It is a light, sweet break for a warm afternoon, or a coffee-free choice at any time of day.

Orzo (barley coffee)

Caffè d'orzo, the roasted barley drink of Italian bars: warm, toasty and round.

Ask staff about allergens

More details

Where it comes from

Roasted barley has been brewed as a warm drink in Italy for generations. It became especially widespread when real coffee was scarce or expensive, as during the world wars, and it stayed on as a familiar alternative long after. Today "un orzo" is a standard order in Italian bars, and many families also keep it at home for breakfast, next to the moka pot.

How it is made

Barley grains are roasted until dark and then ground. At the bar, orzo is prepared with the espresso machine, with dedicated equipment or from a soluble preparation, and it is served just like a coffee. The taste is toasty and slightly sweet, with a softer bitterness than coffee and none of its acidity.

Curiosities

  • You can order it "in tazza piccola", like an espresso, or "in tazza grande", like a larger coffee.
  • It is often combined with milk in the same way as coffee, for a cappuccino d'orzo or an orzo macchiato.
  • Chicory root is another traditional coffee substitute in Italy, and some blends combine the two.

How to enjoy it

Drink it on its own or with a little milk, at breakfast or in the afternoon, when you want something warm and toasty that is not an espresso.

Chinotto al banco

Chinotto, Italy's dark, bittersweet citrus soda, served at the counter.

Allergen details: please confirm with staff

More details

Where it comes from

Chinotto is both a fruit and a drink. The fruit is a small, bitter citrus related to the bitter orange, grown for centuries along the Ligurian coast around Savona, where it was traditionally candied or preserved. In the twentieth century Italian drink makers began using its extracts to flavour a dark, bittersweet soft drink, and chinotto became one of the most recognisable sodas in Italy.

How it is made

In general the drink is made from extracts of the chinotto fruit, often together with other citrus and aromatic herbs, then sweetened and carbonated. Every producer has its own balance, from more bitter and herbal to rounder and sweeter. The result is a dark soda with a citrus bitterness that sets it apart from other soft drinks.

Curiosities

  • Because of its dark colour, chinotto is often described as the Italian alternative to cola, although the taste is quite different.
  • The Chinotto di Savona is protected as a Slow Food Presidium, to safeguard the traditional cultivation of the fruit.

How to enjoy it

Drink it on its own or with something savoury on the side. It is a good choice when you want a soft drink with character and a gentle bitter edge.

Calzone

A small calzone, folded pizza dough with a savoury filling, warmed at our counter.

Ask staff about allergens

More details

Where it comes from The calzone is a child of Naples, the city of pizza. Its name means trouser leg or stocking, a playful nod to its shape: a pizza folded over its filling and closed like a pocket. It belongs to the Neapolitan tradition of food made to be eaten in the street, hot and in the hand.

How it is made A disc of pizza dough is covered with filling on one half, folded into a half-moon and sealed along the edge. In Naples it is baked in the oven or fried, and either way the filling stays inside, hot and melting. Ours is a small calzone, warmed at our counter, and it comes in two variants: ask us which one is available.

Curiosities - In Puglia, a smaller fried cousin of the calzone is called panzerotto. - Because it is closed on all sides, the calzone is easy to eat on the move, one reason it became a street food classic. - The folded shape keeps the heat in for a long time, so take the first bite with care.

How to enjoy it A small, warm snack for a quick lunch or a savoury break in the afternoon, good with a cold drink.

A slice of Genoese-style focaccia with cherry tomatoes, good on its own or with a coffee.

More details

Where it comes from Focaccia genovese comes from Genoa and the Ligurian coast, where in dialect it is simply called fugassa. It is part of everyday life there: bought at the bakery in the morning, eaten on the way to school or work, shared at any hour of the day.

How it is made Traditionally, a soft dough rich in olive oil is stretched into a tray and left to rise. Before baking, the baker presses the surface with the fingertips to make the typical dimples and covers it with an emulsion of oil, water and salt, so the top turns golden while the dimples stay moist. We serve it by the slice, topped with cherry tomatoes.

Curiosities - In Genoa it is common to dip focaccia into a cappuccino or caffellatte at breakfast. - The dimples are not decoration: they hold the oil and the brine, which gives the soft, savoury centre. - Focaccia di Recco, from the same coast, is a completely different product: two thin sheets of dough filled with cheese.

How to enjoy it Try it the Genoese way with a cappuccino in the morning, or later in the day as a savoury snack or next to a salad. The cherry tomatoes add a sweet, juicy note to every bite.

Where it comes from Insalata caprese takes its name from Capri, the island in the Gulf of Naples. It became popular in the twentieth century and is now served all over Italy, above all in summer. Lasagna al banco

Layers of soft pasta with tomato, mozzarella and minced meat, served at our counter.

Allergen details: please confirm with staff

More details

Where it comes from Lasagne is one of the oldest pasta shapes in Italy. The word is usually traced back to the Greek laganon and the Latin lasanum, and wide sheets of dough layered with sauce already appear in medieval Italian cookbooks. Today almost every region has its own version: in Emilia-Romagna the classic is built with ragù and béchamel, while in Naples lasagna is a Carnival dish, rich with ricotta and small meatballs.

How it is made Sheets of pasta are stacked with a filling between each layer, then the dish is baked until the top sets and the edges turn golden. Once cut, every portion shows its layers on the side. Our lasagna al banco is made of soft layers of pasta with tomato, mozzarella and minced meat.

Curiosities - In the north the dish is often called lasagne, in the plural, while in the south you mostly hear lasagna. - Many Italian cooks say lasagna is even better after resting, when the layers have settled and the flavours have come together. - In Naples, lasagna di Carnevale is traditionally served on Martedì Grasso, the last feast day before Lent.

How to enjoy it Al banco means at the counter: this is lasagna as a proper lunch, eaten at your own pace. Finish with an espresso, the way many Italians close a meal.

Parmigiana al banco

Layers of fried aubergine with mozzarella and tomato, a southern Italian classic at our counter.

Allergen details: please confirm with staff

More details

Where it comes from Parmigiana di melanzane is a classic of southern Italy, claimed with equal pride by Campania and Sicily. The name is debated: some link it to Parmigiano cheese, others to the Sicilian word parmiciana, the slats of a wooden shutter, which overlap just like the layers of aubergine.

How it is made Aubergines are sliced, fried and layered in a dish with tomato sauce and cheese, then baked until everything melts together. Every family has its own details, from how thin to cut the aubergine to whether to dip it in flour or egg before frying. Our parmigiana al banco is made of layers of fried aubergine with mozzarella and tomato.

Curiosities - Despite the name, most food historians place the dish in the south rather than in Parma. - Parmigiana is often said to taste even better the next day, once the layers have settled. - In many southern homes it is eaten warm or at room temperature, not only straight from the oven.

How to enjoy it At our counter it is a full lunch on its own: take your time, let the tomato and the aubergine do the talking, and finish with an espresso.

Polpette di vitello in salsa di pomodoro

Veal meatballs in tomato sauce, a comforting dish from the Italian family table.

Ask staff about allergens

More details

Where it comes from Polpette, meatballs, are cooked in every corner of Italy, and almost every family has its own way of making them. Where it comes from

Torta caprese takes its name from Capri, the island in the Gulf of Naples, and it is one of the best-known cakes of Campania. It is usually dated to the early twentieth century and has since become a fixture in pastry shops and home kitchens across southern Italy, as well as a favourite souvenir for visitors to the island.

How it is made

Dark chocolate and ground almonds are the heart of the classic recipe. They are combined into a rich batter and baked into a low, round cake. The outside forms a thin, slightly crisp crust, while the inside stays dense, moist and deeply chocolatey. A dusting of icing sugar is the traditional finish.

Curiosities

How to enjoy it

Enjoy it at room temperature with an espresso in the morning or a cappuccino in the afternoon. It is rich, so take your time with it and let each bite melt slowly.

Where it comes from

Ricotta e pera is a recent classic from the Amalfi Coast. It was created by a pastry chef from the area in the 1990s and was quickly adopted by pastry shops across Italy, becoming one of the best-known modern Italian cakes. It brings together two ingredients that Italians have paired for centuries: fresh cheese and pear.

How it is made

The classic version layers a delicate ricotta cream with small pieces of pear, cooked briefly with sugar, between two thin, soft sponge discs. The cake is kept cool so the cream stays firm and is dusted with icing sugar before serving. Every slice balances creamy, fruity and light.

Curiosities

  • An old Italian proverb says 'al contadino non far sapere quanto è buono il formaggio con le pere': don't let the farmer know how good cheese is with pears.
  • Despite being so young, it is now found in pastry shops from Naples to Milan.

How to enjoy it

Enjoy it cool with a small fork, next to an espresso or a cup of tea. It makes a good dessert after lunch and a quiet treat with an afternoon coffee. Take it slowly: ricotta and pear are delicate flavours that reward a little attention.

Zeppola di San Giuseppe

Choux pastry with custard cream and a sour cherry, the traditional sweet of Saint Joseph's Day.

Allergen details: please confirm with staff

More details

Where it comes from

The zeppola di San Giuseppe belongs to 19 March, Saint Joseph's Day, which is also Father's Day in Italy. It is especially associated with Naples and Campania, while similar pastries are made across southern Italy for the same feast. The Neapolitan cook Ippolito Cavalcanti is often cited for writing down a recipe for zeppole in his cookbook of 1837, a sign of how old the custom is.

How it is made

It is made from choux dough piped into a ring and then fried or baked, depending on the tradition. The ring is filled and topped with crema pasticcera, finished with an amarena, a sour cherry in syrup, and dusted with icing sugar.

Curiosities

  • According to popular tradition, Saint Joseph sold fritters to support his family during the flight into Egypt, which is why fried sweets are linked to his feast.
  • In Rome and Lazio the same day brings bignè di San Giuseppe, round choux puffs filled with cream.
  • In Italy, children often give their fathers zeppole on 19 March.

How to enjoy it

Around mid-March it is a must, but it works any time with an espresso. Eat it with a small fork so the cream stays where it belongs.
